| The disk's inner edge moves at half the speed of light (said in round 6; cut from the narration in round 7, kept here because the Doppler row relies on it) | True for a thin disk around a non-spinning black hole: its inner edge sits at the innermost stable circular orbit (3 Schwarzschild radii), where a circular orbit's speed measured by a local static observer is v = c·√(M/(r − 2M)) = c/2. | "The black hole bends its light, up over the top... and under the bottom." (the disk's back half) | True: the far side of the disk is imaged both above the shadow and, as a thinner arc, below it; both are visible in the render (B). | Luminet (1979); James et al. (2015), above |

| The sky above you is brighter and bluer at the photon sphere (shown) | True: infalling light is gravitationally blueshifted for a static observer by 1/√(1 − r_s/r). | APOD 2013 July 2 above ("blueshifted, brightened sky images"); Wikipedia, "Gravitational redshift", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_redshift (1 + z = 1/√(1 − r_s/R)) |

| "And down here, time runs slower. Stay for one minute... and half an hour goes by out there!" / "1 MIN DOWN HERE" → "32 MIN OUT THERE" (at 1.001× the horizon radius) | True for a hovering (static) observer: gravitational time dilation makes their clock run slow by √(1 − r_s/r), so one of their minutes is 1/√(1 − 1/1.001) = 31.6 minutes far away ("half an hour" said, 32 shown rounded). **What I simplified, and where.** The disk is thin and non-spinning (Schwarzschild), and the disk colours are blackbody colours scaled into the visible range (a real disk this close would peak in X-rays). The cyan tint on the disk's far half in shot B is a highlight to lead the eye, not a physical colour. The "you" in shot E is a diagram (labelled). The camera in the dive (C) is rendered as a sequence of hovering observers, so it doesn't include the aberration a moving observer would see.
