## Fact-check: every claim in the script

| claim (as said or shown) | verdict | source |
|---|---|---|
| The disk's far side shows up arching over the top of the black hole ("That's the back of the disk, bent over the top by gravity.") | True: strong gravitational lensing images the far side of a thin disk above (and below) the shadow when seen from near the disk plane. First computed by Luminet (1979) and rendered for *Interstellar*. | J.-P. Luminet, "Image of a spherical black hole with thin accretion disk", *Astron. Astrophys.* 75, 228 (1979), described in Luminet, "40 Years of Black Hole Imaging (1)", https://blogs.futura-sciences.com/e-luminet/2018/03/07/45-years-black-hole-imaging-1-early-work-1972-1988/ ; O. James, E. von Tunzelmann, P. Franklin, K. S. Thorne, "Gravitational lensing by spinning black holes in astrophysics, and in the movie Interstellar", *Class. Quantum Grav.* 32, 065001 (2015), https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015CQGra..32f5001J/abstract |
| "That disk is actually flat." | A simplification: our simulation uses a geometrically thin disk, the standard model for bright accretion disks. Real disks can be thicker. Shown as the simulation's disk, not as a claim about every black hole. | Luminet (1979) above (thin-disk model) |
| The disk's inner edge sits at 3× the horizon radius (shown, not said) | True for a non-spinning black hole: the innermost stable circular orbit is at 3 Schwarzschild radii, and the disk's inner edge is expected there. | A. J. S. Hamilton, "Journey into a Schwarzschild black hole", JILA, https://jila.colorado.edu/~ajsh/insidebh/schw.html ("3 Schwarzschild radii marks the radius of the innermost stable orbit") |
| One side of the disk is brighter and bluer (shown) | True: Doppler beaming. Gas moving toward the viewer at up to half the speed of light (at the inner edge) is boosted and blueshifted. Rendered with the frequency ratio g and intensity ∝ g⁴. | Luminet (1979); Hamilton, JILA page above |
| "Here, light goes in circles." (at the photon sphere, 1.5× on the distance counter) | True: at 1.5 Schwarzschild radii (the photon sphere) light can travel in (unstable) circular orbits. | Hamilton, JILA page above ("At 1.5 Schwarzschild radii is a special location, the photon sphere, where light rays can orbit in circular orbits"); Wikipedia, "Photon sphere", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_sphere (r = 3GM/c² = 3r_s/2) |
| The disk's inner edge moves at half the speed of light (said in round 6; cut from the narration in round 7, kept here because the Doppler row relies on it) | True for a thin disk around a non-spinning black hole: its inner edge sits at the innermost stable circular orbit (3 Schwarzschild radii), where a circular orbit's speed measured by a local static observer is v = c·√(M/(r − 2M)) = c/2. (Farther out the gas is slower, about 0.2c at the disk's outer edge in the render.) | Wikipedia, "Innermost stable circular orbit", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innermost_stable_circular_orbit (r = 6GM/c² = 3 r_s); ISCO speed of c/2 stated in e.g. arXiv:2110.05764, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.05764 |
| "…bent over the top by gravity, and under the bottom." | True: the far side of the disk is imaged both above the shadow and, as a thinner arc, below it; both are visible in the render (B). | Luminet (1979); James et al. (2015), above |
| "Hover here, and the black hole fills exactly half your sky." | True for an observer holding position (static) at the photon sphere. The words "hover here" state the condition. | R. Nemiroff, APOD 2013 July 2, "Circling a Black Hole at its Photon Sphere", https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap130702.html ("half of the sky would appear completely black, half of the sky would appear unusually bright") |
| "That line? The back of your own head." (the light that went once round, arriving back at the viewer) | True in principle: light leaving the back of your head can go once around and come back into your eyes (very distorted and thin). | APOD 2013 July 2 above ("the back of your head would appear across the middle"); Wikipedia, "Photon sphere" ("allowing one to see the back of the head (however very distorted)") |
| The sky above you is brighter and bluer at the photon sphere (shown) | True: infalling light is gravitationally blueshifted for a static observer by 1/√(1 − r_s/r). | APOD 2013 July 2 above ("blueshifted, brightened sky images"); Wikipedia, "Gravitational redshift", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_redshift (1 + z = 1/√(1 − r_s/R)) |
| "Now hover just above the horizon, and the whole universe shrinks to one dot overhead." | True for a **hovering** observer (rockets holding position). It is *not* what a free-falling observer sees, which is why the script says "hover" and never "fall in". | Hamilton, JILA page above ("if you lower yourself very slowly towards the horizon, firing your rockets like crazy just to stay put, then indeed your view of the outside universe will be concentrated into a small, bright circle above you") |
| The dot's size at each distance (91° across at 1.1×, 30° at 1.01×, 9.4° at 1.001×) | Computed from the escape cone for a static observer, sin ψ = (3√3/2)(r_s/r)√(1 − r_s/r) | J. L. Synge, "The Escape of Photons from Gravitationally Intense Stars", *MNRAS* 131, 463 (1966), in modern notation in A. Ogasawara et al., "Escape, bound and capture geodesics in local static coordinates in Schwarzschild spacetime", https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.12486 |
| The distance counter's readings (20× → 1.5× → 1.001× the horizon radius) | Shown exactly as rendered: the counter reads each frame's camera radius in Schwarzschild radii (horizon = 1×). 1.5× is the photon sphere and 1.001× is 0.1% above the horizon, as labelled. | Hamilton; Wikipedia "Photon sphere" (above) |
| "And down here, time runs slower. Stay for one minute... and half an hour goes by out there." / "1 MIN DOWN HERE" → "32 MIN OUT THERE" (at 1.001× the horizon radius) | True for a hovering (static) observer: gravitational time dilation makes their clock run slow by √(1 − r_s/r), so one of their minutes is 1/√(1 − 1/1.001) = 31.6 minutes far away ("half an hour" said, 32 shown rounded). The same factor blueshifts the starlight. | Wikipedia, "Gravitational time dilation", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_time_dilation (t₀ = t_f √(1 − r_s/r)); "Gravitational redshift" (above) |
| The line across the sky at the photon sphere shown opening into the back of your own head (E2, labelled "* MAGNIFIED ILLUSTRATION") | The physics is true: light from the back of your head can circle once and arrive from straight ahead, so the line holds an image of you from behind (see the rows above). The picture is magnified. For real, that image is a hair-thin sliver stretched around the whole line, not a readable helmet. | APOD 2013 July 2; Wikipedia, "Photon sphere" (above) |
| "All that darkness around it? That's the black hole." | True for the hovering observer: every direction outside the escape cone traces back into the horizon, so no outside light arrives from there. | Hamilton; Synge (above) |
| The thin bright ring hugging the shadow (the motif) is light that looped around the hole, and each thinner inner ring is another, smaller image of the whole sky (shown, not said) | True. | M. D. Johnson et al., "Universal interferometric signatures of a black hole's photon ring", *Science Advances* 6, eaaz1310 (2020), https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aaz1310 ("each subring contains a separate, exponentially demagnified image of the entire universe") |
| "REAL PHYSICS SIMULATION" | True of our renders: every pixel of shots A–D and F–G is a null geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric traced numerically, with Doppler and gravitational frequency shifts. Shot E is labelled as a diagram. | this repo, `shorts/blackhole/` |

**What I simplified, and where.** The disk is thin and non-spinning (Schwarzschild), and the disk colours are blackbody colours scaled into the visible range (a real disk this close would peak in X-rays). The cyan tint on the disk's far half in shot B is a highlight to lead the eye, not a physical colour. The "you" in shot E is a diagram (labelled). The camera in the dive (C) is rendered as a sequence of hovering observers, so it doesn't include the aberration a moving observer would see.
